MATH2100 - Algebra [Fall + Winter]

Ju Hong Kim · September 5, 2099

university   school   math   reviews   carletonu

Report a bug

This is a course review of MATH2100 - Algebra taken at CarletonU in the fall of 2022 and winter 2023. The lectures and tutorial were synchronous and in-person. Similarly to MATH2000, the course is split into two semesters covering different topics. The fall semester goes into a rabbit hole in the topic of Linear Algebra and the winter semester does an introduction to Group Theory and Rings.

TLDR:

The review is split into 2 main parts:

  1. Long Course Description: In-depth talk about the course topic from how I understood it
    • Fall Term - Linear Algebra but in-depth
    • Winter Term - Introduction to Group Theory and Rings
  2. Course Review: Solely focuses on the teaching style, difficulty, and anything that would be of interest

Professor: Paul Mezo

Course Delivery: In-person lectures with synchronous tutorials

Class Size:

  • 38 students (October 25 2022) Test 1 Statistics
  • 35 students (December 2022) Mid-year Exam Statistics
  • 33 students (February 21 2023) Test 2 Statstics
  • 30 students (March 28 2023)

Course Description (from website): Introduction to group theory: permutation groups, Lagrange’s theorem, normal subgroups, homomorphism theorems. Introduction to ring theory: ring of polynomials, integral domains, ideals, homomorphism theorems. Hermitian forms, spectral theorem for normal operators, bilinear and quadratic forms, classical groups.

Course Description (LONG) with Commentary:

Fall Term - Linear Algebra But In-Depth

Similarly to first year calculus (MATH1152, MATH2152), the lectures goes over a lot of proofs. As stated at the beginning, the course is split into two semesters covering two distinct topics. In the Fall, it is a continuation of Linear Algebra except in much more depth and rigor and in the winter, an introduction to Group Theory and Rings is given. The course begins with a revisit of everything we learned in first year and it surprisingly goes in-depth. Although I did not take MATH2152, I was confident that I had a decent understanding of Linear Algebra, having done very well in MATH1152 and MATH2107 (non-honors linear algebra). However, I quickly learned how wrong I was.

The course begins as any rigorous Linear Algebra course would, going over Vector Space Properties. One cannot start a game without first understanding the rules or else players would cheat unintentionally and cause chaos. This is why we should always begin defining what is a vector and what we can do with vectors.

In Physics, a vector would some quantity that has both a size (a magnitude) and a direction. However, in Mathematics, we view a vector a bit more abstractly as a list of size n where each number belongs to some field $F$. For now let’s define a vector as the following (Mathematicians, please don’t murder me over this):

Vector: a vector $v$ of dimension $n$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is a n-ordered tuple in the form $\vec{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ where $v_i \in \mathbb{F}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$

Example:

  • 2D Cartesian Plane over the Reals (Euclidean Plane): $\mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x, y): a, b \in\mathbb{R}\}$
    • e.g. (4, 2) or (1, 0)
  • 2D Cartesian Plane over the Complex: $\mathbb{C}^2 = \{(a, b): a, b \in \mathbb{C}\}$
    • e.g. (4 + 2i, 1 - i)

Now you may be confused because a vector looks exactly like a coordinate, at least for the vectors on the Euclidean plane. Certainly for any vector $v = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$, there is also a point $p = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ that have the same representation. Geometrically, they appear different in some visualizable space such as $\mathbb{R}^2$ (where we usually draw the vector to be some displacement rather than a single point such as having its tail be at the the origin), we will treat them as the same and say $v = p$ assuming both $v$ and $p$ belong to the same vector space (i.e. you cannot compare apples to oranges if $v$ and $p$ have different sets of rules applied to them). Now is a good time to explain what a vector space is:

Vector Space: a vector space is a set $V = \{(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n): v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in F\}$ with two operations (addition and scalar multiplication) such that they respect the following properties:

  1. Commutativity: $u + v = v + u$, $\forall u, v \in V$
  2. Associativity: $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$ and $(c_1c_2)v = c_1(c_2v)$, $\forall u, v, w \in V$ and $\forall c_1, c_2 \in F$
  3. Additive Identity: $\exists$ an element $\textbf{0}\in V$ such that $v + \textbf{0} = v$, $\forall v\in{V}$
  4. Additive Inverse: $\forall v\in V, \exists w \in V$ such that $v + w = \textbf{0}$
  5. Multiplicative Identity: $1v = v$, $\forall v \in V$ and $1\in F$
  6. Distributive Property: $c_1(u+v) = c_1u + c_1v$ and $(c_1 + c_2)v = c_1v + c_2v$, $\forall c_1, c_2\in F$ and $\forall u, v \in V$

Example:

  • $\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{C}^n$
  • $\mathbb{R}[x]$: the vectorspace with a set of polynomial with real coefficients e.g. $1+x \in \mathbb{R}[x]$
  • $\mathbb{C}[x]$: the complex polynomials e.g. $1+ix \in \mathbb{C}[x]$
  • $M_{mn}(F)$: a vector space consisting of $m \times n$ matrices with entries in F

Now that we have defined a vector space, we can now redine a vector to simply be an element in the vector space.

Review

Note: The content will differ depending on who teaches the course and this review has been written in 2025, 2 years after the course has been taken so my memory is a bit hazy.

MATH2100 with Mezo has been a delightful experience. I enjoyed the way Mezo taught the course to the point I decided to take MATH3106 - Group Theory despite hating group theory. As the class was small, the course was much more interactive. As MATH2100 was a two semester course (at least when I took it), it was nicely divided into two parts: linear algebra and group theory.

Grading Scheme:

  • 10% Assignments
  • 30% Tests (x2)
  • 30% Mid-year exam
  • 30% Final exam

The first half was enlightening and makes you humble if you thought you had a decent understanding of Linear Algebra.
